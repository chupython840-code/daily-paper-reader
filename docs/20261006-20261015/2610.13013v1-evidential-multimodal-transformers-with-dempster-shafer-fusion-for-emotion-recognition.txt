# Evidential Multimodal Transformers with Dempster-Shafer Fusion for Emotion Recognition 

Muhammad Usama ∗† and Yeoreum Jung ∗

Abstract —Multimodal emotion recognition faces the challenge of overconfidence, where standard softmax-based methods assign high probability even when sensor data is corrupted or contradic-tory. For instance, facial expressions may suggest positive valence while vocal prosody indicates negative arousal, creating semantic conflict that standard averaging suppresses. We present an Evidential Multimodal Transformer with Dempster-Shafer Fusion (EMT-DS) that addresses this problem through formal uncertainty quantification. EMT-DS models predictions as Subjective Opinions (Dirichlet distributions) rather than point estimates, separating epistemic uncertainty (lack of evidence) from aleatoric uncertainty (sensor noise). Modality-specific transformer encoders generate belief mass distributions, which we fuse using Dempster’s Rule of Combination. This mechanism mathematically distinguishes agreement from conflict by normalizing the joint belief mass with a conflict coefficient ( 1/(1 − c)), so that contradictory evidence is explicitly quantified as conflict and the fused decision probability becomes diffuse rather than being forced into an unjustified consensus. We demonstrate that EMT-DS achieves consistent performance gains across four benchmark datasets under subject-independent and speaker-independent evaluation. On IEMOCAP (audio+text), EMT-DS achieves 84.3% accuracy versus 81.7% for the best base-line DCT (+2.6 points, p < 0.01 ). On DEAP (EEG+physiological), under leave-one-subject-out evaluation we obtain 82.4% accuracy versus 78.1% for the strongest baseline (+4.3 points). On the highly imbalanced KEMDy20, EMT-DS reaches 96.8% accuracy, and on the large-scale CMU-MOSEI benchmark it improves accuracy by 2.4 points over the strongest evidential baseline. Through controlled ablation, we demonstrate that EMT-DS reduces Expected Calibration Error from 0.153 (standard softmax transformer) to 0.042. Furthermore, the model maintains 71.2% accuracy under severe noise ( σ = 1.0 additive Gaussian corruption on audio features) where attention baselines drop to 45.3%, and degrades gracefully when entire modalities are absent. These gains are achieved with O(MK 2) fusion complexity, significantly lower than the O(M2d2) of cross-modal attention. Finally, we demonstrate that the learned conflict metric effectively flags potential errors, enabling a downstream selective-review strategy that captures 80% of misclassifications by reviewing just 20% of samples. This capability to reliably reject unsafe predictions is critical for deployment in clinical and safety-critical systems.

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Emotion recognition systems enable advanced human-computer interaction in mental health monitoring, intelligent tutoring, autonomous vehicles, and social robotics [1], [2]. Human emotions manifest across multiple modalities: facial expressions convey valence, vocal prosody signals arousal, physiological signals reveal autonomic responses, and linguistic content provides semantic context [3], [4]. Multimodal systems integrating these heterogeneous streams consistently outperform unimodal approaches [5], [6]. 

A. Problem Definition 

Despite advances in transformer-based multimodal fusion [7]– [9], existing methods face a fundamental problem: the inability to represent ignorance. We distinguish the task of emotion recognition (mapping sensors to labels) from the core problem of uncertainty quantification. Standard fusion methods, such as concatenation, attention [10], [11], or mixture-of-experts [12], [13], map inputs to a probability distribution on the simplex 

∆K−1. The obstacle is the softmax normalization constraint 

∑ πk = 1, which forces a deterministic distribution even when evidence is weak or conflicting. This “forced confidence” leads to brittle failure modes: (1) sensor failure where noise is interpreted as a confident prediction; (2) semantic conflict where contradictory modalities (e.g., sarcasm) are averaged out rather than flagged. Our solution is to reformulate the output as a Dirichlet distribution over the simplex, explicitly separating belief from uncertainty using Subjective Logic. We present Evidential Multimodal Transformer with Dempster-Shafer Fusion (EMT-DS), integrating these frame-works. First, each modality is processed by specialized transformer encoders [14]–[16] followed by evidential layers predicting Dirichlet parameters. The loss combines Bayes risk with KL regularization that penalizes overconfidence on noisy inputs [17]. Second, a differentiable Dempster-Shafer (D-S) fusion layer computes belief masses, uncertainty, and conflict for each modality pair. Unlike attention mechanisms that learn arbitrary weights [10], our fusion follows mathematically grounded belief combination semantics, explicitly using conflict to detect complex emotional states or sensor failures. Third, we show that under low conflict, fused uncertainty decreases below individual modalities, while cross-modal disagreement is explicitly quantified as conflict and drives the fused decision probability toward a non-committal state, providing robustness. The KL regularizer also drives the evidence toward the vacuous opinion for out-of-distribution (OOD) samples, so that gradients vanish and noise is not fit.

We delineate the novelty of EMT-DS precisely. Evidential deep learning and Subjective Logic are established tools that we build upon rather than claim as contributions. The technical novelty lies in three elements. First, we design a fully differentiable Dempster-Shafer fusion layer that backpropagates gradients through the conflict coefficient, in contrast to prior Dempster-Shafer emotion-recognition methods [18]–[20] that apply combination rules post hoc to the outputs of separately trained classifiers. Second, we treat the cross-modal conflict as an explicit supervised training signal and as a deployable abstention score for human review, rather than as a quantity to be normalized away. Third, we provide a theoretical account connecting the fusion to Bayes-optimal inference and connecting the evidential regularizer to out-of-distribution uncertainty, and we validate it under subject-independent, missing-modality, and backbone-agnostic protocols. Unlike attention or gating mechanisms that learn weights with no fixed semantics [10], [12], the belief and conflict quantities in EMT-DS carry a fixed probabilistic meaning and make cross-modal disagreement explicit through the conflict mass. Validation on IEMOCAP [21], DEAP [22], KEMDy20 [23], and the large-scale CMU-MOSEI [24] benchmark shows EMT-DS outperforms transformer-based [5], [6], [25], mixture-of-experts [13], evidential [26], and cross-modal attention [10], [27] methods by 2.3 to 4.3 percentage points in accuracy and 2.4 to 4.2 points in macro F1-score on the subject-independent and speaker-independent benchmarks (IEMOCAP, DEAP, and KEMDy20), and remains ahead of the strongest recent 2026 baseline on the large-scale CMU-MOSEI corpus, while reducing Expected Calibration Error by up to a factor of three. Learned conflict patterns reveal systematic cross-modal dissonance in specific emotion pairs, providing interpretable insights. Section II reviews related work. Section III presents the math-ematical formulation. Section IV describes datasets, reports results, comparisons, and provides detailed ablation studies. Section V analyzes key findings. Section VI concludes with implications and future directions. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Multimodal Emotion Recognition 

Multimodal emotion recognition has evolved from early concatenation [3] to deep learning fusion. Early fusion con-catenates low-level features [4], enabling joint representations but requiring synchronization. Late fusion combines classifier outputs [28], providing robustness to missing modalities but discarding low-level interactions. Hybrid approaches [19] balance both strategies. Attention-based fusion [10], [11] dynamically weights modalities, but lacks a formal mechanism to reject disagreements. Transformer architectures [7] dominate recent work. Le et al. [5] proposed transformer fusion with emotion-level representation learning. Zhao et al. [6] introduced TDFNet with deep-scale fusion across temporal resolutions. Maji et al. [10] combined cross-modal transformers with temporal self-attention. Tsai et al. [25] introduced the Multimodal Transformer (MulT) with directional pairwise cross-modal attention for unaligned sequences, which remains a strong general-purpose baseline. Liu et al. [9] proposed TACFN with adaptive cross-modal fusion, while Yi et al. [29] introduced dual cross-modal attention. Beyond attention, recent conversational emotion-recognition methods model richer interaction structure: Shi et al. [30] drive multimodal fusion with speaker-identity and modality attributes through a speaker-centric graph, and Chen and Shi [31] construct dynamic monologic and dialogic sub-hypergraphs to capture interaction-pattern-specific emotional relationships. These graph- and attribute-based designs improve accuracy by structuring cross-utterance interactions, but, like the attention and mixture-of-experts models, they produce softmax outputs without a formal account of uncertainty or cross-modal conflict; EMT-DS is complementary, contributing belief and conflict quantities with fixed probabilistic meaning that could augment such architectures. Mixture-of-experts approaches like FuseMoE [12] and CAG-MoE [13] provide modality special-ization with sparse activation. However, all these methods rely on softmax outputs lacking formal uncertainty quantification, making them brittle when modalities conflict or sensors fail, the gap that EMT-DS addresses through evidential Dirichlet modeling and conflict-aware Dempster-Shafer fusion. Research in 2026 has continued to pursue robustness and reliability in multimodal fusion. Wei et al. [32] debias dynamic fusion with a noise-aware uncertainty estimator, Huang et al. [33] route spectral bands under complementarity supervision, and Zhang et al. [34] combine dynamic weighting with Dempster-Shafer evidence for trustworthy dual-modal inference. Closest to our setting, Grzeczkowicz et al. [35] also pair Dempster-Shafer theory with Dirichlet evidence for emotion recognition, but as a training-free procedure applied post hoc to the logits of a frozen model. EMT-DS differs in that its Dempster-Shafer fusion is learned end to end, with gradients flowing through the conflict coefficient, with conflict serving as an explicit supervised signal and abstention score, and with the accompanying calibration and out-of-distribution analysis that a post-hoc wrapper does not provide. 

B. Physiological-Based Emotion Recognition 

Physiological signals (EEG, EDA, HRV) directly measure autonomic responses to emotional stimuli [36], [37]. On the DEAP benchmark [22], Sartipi et al. [38] proposed spatiotemporal attention with graph-based smoothing. Yin et al. [39] demonstrated complementarity between EEG and electrooculography. Zhang et al. [27] introduced cross-modal credibility modeling for EEG systems with learned reliability scores. These credibility measures are heuristic rather than grounded in formal uncertainty frameworks, unlike EMT-DS’s Subjective Logic foundation. 

C. Evidential Deep Learning and Subjective Logic 

Evidential deep learning [17] reformulates classification as predicting Dirichlet parameters, with strength directly quantifying epistemic uncertainty. Li et al. [40] extended this to hierarchical settings, while Li et al. [41] applied it to medical segmentation with improved noise robustness. Subjective Logic [42] provides formal algebra for belief IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON AFFECTIVE COMPUTING 3

reasoning through opinions ω = ( b, u, a) with belief masses, uncertainty, and base rates. Koksalmis and Kabak [43] applied Dempster-Shafer combination to large-scale sensor fusion, showing that explicit conflict handling improves classification reliability. Recent work explored evidential multimodal fusion in other domains. Han et al. [26] proposed trusted multi-view classifica-tion, which characterizes each view by a Dirichlet distribution and integrates views through Dempster-Shafer combination; it is the closest evidential-fusion baseline to our setting, although it targets generic multi-view inputs and does not model temporal emotional dynamics or treat conflict as a supervised signal. Huang et al. [44] proposed deep evidential fusion for medical segmentation. Mohey El-Din et al. [45] developed adaptive fusion using Dempster-Shafer theory for visual-textual object recognition. However, these approaches focus on static images rather than temporal emotion dynamics. EMT-DS uniquely integrates evidential transformers with differentiable Dempster-Shafer fusion for emotion recognition, enabling end-to-end learning where conflict signals complex emotional states rather than being discarded. 

D. Uncertainty Quantification in Deep Learning 

Bayesian neural networks [46] and ensemble methods [47] enable uncertainty estimation but require multiple forward passes, increasing cost. Gal and Ghahramani [46] interpreted dropout as Bayesian approximation for Monte Carlo sampling. Kendall and Gal [47] distinguished aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty for safety-critical applications. Evidential methods offer single-pass uncertainty through predictive distribution parameterization. Pandey and Yu [48] showed evidential net-works accumulate evidence across training samples, improving generalization. EMT-DS leverages this for multimodal emotion recognition, where evidential encoding captures varying modal-ity reliability while Dempster-Shafer fusion provides formal conflict resolution. 

E. Dempster-Shafer Theory in Emotion Recognition 

Dempster-Shafer Theory [49], [50] has limited application to emotion recognition. Xu et al. [18] derived belief assignments from classifier confidences for multimodal video emotion recognition. Nemati et al. [19] employed weighted belief combination in hybrid latent fusion. Soroush et al. [20], [51] applied modified Dempster-Shafer theory to EEG recognition with nonlinear features. These treat Dempster-Shafer as post-hoc fusion on pre-trained outputs. EMT-DS differs in three concrete respects: (1) Dirichlet-parameterized evidential layers are embedded in the architecture for end-to-end belief learning; (2) differentiable Dempster-Shafer fusion backpropagates gradients through conflict com-putation; (3) conflict serves as an explicit signal for complex emotional states rather than being normalized away. This enables learning when and why modalities conflict, providing both improved accuracy (2.3 to 4.3 percentage points on the subject-independent and speaker-independent benchmarks) and interpretable uncertainty estimates. We employ transformer-based encoders tailored to the characteristics of each input modality, followed by an evidential head that generates Subjective Opinions. We employ transformer-based encoders to capture long-range temporal dependencies inherent in emotional expression (e.g., prosodic contours in audio or micro-expressions in video) which are often missed by local convolution operations. C. Dempster-Shafer Fusion 

Given Subjective Opinions ω1, . . . , ωM from M modalities, we must fuse them into a joint opinion ωfused . Traditional approaches such as weighted averaging or concatenation do not provide a mechanism for handling contradictory evidence. We employ Dempster’s Rule of Combination [49], [50], which explicitly computes the conflict between opinions and adjusts the fused belief accordingly. We favor Dempster-Shafer over Yager or simple Bayesian fusion because it strictly penalizes conflicting evidence through the normalization term, providing a mathematically grounded rejection of inconsistent multimodal inputs (e.g., in cases of sensor failure or sarcasm) rather than averaging them out. This contrasts with Yager’s rule, which redistributes conflict to the universal set (uncertainty) without re-weighting the beliefs, or Bayesian averaging which ignores conflict entirely. E. Theoretical Analysis 

We establish three theoretical results for EMT-DS, stating ex-plicitly the assumptions each relies on and separating them from the empirical observations they explain. First, we show that the evidential parameterization removes the fusion divergence associated with Zadeh’s Paradox, with the required evidence bound guaranteed by construction through an architectural cap (Eq. 6). Second, we show that evidential Dempster-Shafer fusion is Bayes-consistent under conditional independence, which explains our empirical Expected Calibration Error reduction under weak cross-modal error correlation. Third, we show that the KL regularizer is uniquely minimized by the vacuous opinion, encoding a preference for maximum uncertainty in the absence of evidence, and we corroborate the resulting out-of-distribution behavior empirically. 

1) Unconditional Stability via an Architectural Evidence Cap: A fundamental critique of classical Dempster-Shafer theory is Zadeh’s Paradox [54], which demonstrates that when conflict c → 1, the normalization term n = 1/(1 − c) → ∞. This causes the fusion operator to become discontinuous and hyper-sensitive to noise, severely limiting its use in deep learning. We show that the Dirichlet-parameterized evidential mapping removes this singularity. The required evidence bound is not left to chance: the L1 projection in Eq. 6 guarantees ∥em∥1 ≤

Emax for every input by construction, so the stability below is unconditional rather than an empirical regularity. The softplus activation and KL regularization additionally keep evidence magnitudes well within this cap in practice (measured ∥em∥1 ≈

100 ≪ Emax , Section IV-K), so the projection is inactive on observed data while the bound it provides remains permanently valid.

Theorem III.3 and Lemma III.3 act in opposite directions: on labeled, informative samples the Bayes risk drives evidence upward, while on inputs without discriminative signal the KL term is minimized only by the vacuous opinion, so the training objective prefers maximum uncertainty exactly where evidence is absent. We do not claim that an arbitrary out-of-distribution input is forced to this state at test time; rather, we observe empirically (Section IV-K) that as corruption increases the predicted uncertainty rises toward one and the KL-gradient norm collapses, consistent with this design. This section presents a comprehensive empirical evaluation of EMT-DS across four benchmark datasets. We begin by describing the benchmark datasets, followed by experimental setup details, quantitative comparisons with existing baselines, and an in-depth ablation study analyzing the contribution of each architectural component. 

A. Benchmark Datasets 

We evaluate EMT-DS on four widely used multimodal emotion recognition benchmarks that collectively span diverse modalities, cultural contexts, scales, and emotional taxonomies. IEMOCAP [21] contains approximately 12 hours of audio-visual data from 10 professional actors engaged in scripted IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON AFFECTIVE COMPUTING 9

and improvised dyadic interactions. Following standard prac-tice [55], [56], we adopt the 4-class protocol (Neutral, Happy, Sad, Angry), in which the Excited class is merged into Happy, giving a corpus of 5,531 utterances with an approximately balanced distribution. We use audio (Wav2Vec2 [15]) and text (BERT [16]) modalities with 5-fold session-independent cross-validation, where the five recording sessions form the five folds; in each fold one session is held out for testing and a second for validation, so no speaker appears in both training and test. DEAP [22] comprises physiological recordings from 32 participants who watched 40 one-minute music videos. The dataset provides 32-channel EEG (128 Hz) and 8 peripheral physiological signals (EDA, respiration, BVP, temperature). Participants rated each video on 1-9 scales for Valence and Arousal. We threshold ratings at 5.0 to create four valence-arousal quadrant classes (LALV, HALV, LAHV, HAHV), using EEG and peripheral signals with subject-independent leave-one-subject-out cross-validation. This corrects the subject-dependent protocol used in some prior work, which allows trials from the same participant to appear in both training and test and inflates accuracy; under our protocol every test subject is entirely unseen during training. KEMDy20 [13], [23] contains data from 80 Korean-speaking participants (40 dyadic pairs) engaged in naturalistic conver-sations, providing four modalities: audio, text, physiological signals (EDA/IBI/temperature via Empatica E4), and video. We retain five emotion classes (Neutral, Happy, Sad, Angry, Surprise) and exclude Disgust and Fear because they contain too few samples for reliable speaker-independent evaluation (157 utterances combined, under 1.2% of the corpus), following common practice on this Neutral-dominated dataset. The re-tained classes exhibit severe imbalance, with Neutral and Happy accounting for roughly 95% of samples; the per-class counts are Neutral 9,603, Happy 3,147, Sad 301, Angry 228, and Surprise 161 (13,440 utterances). We use audio (Wav2Vec2), text (KoELECTRA [52]), physiological (1D-CNN), and video (ViT) features with 5-fold speaker-independent cross-validation, verified so that no speaker appears in more than one fold. All KEMDy20 results, tables, and the confusion matrix use this five-class protocol. CMU-MOSEI [24] is a large-scale, speaker-diverse bench-mark of about 23,000 utterances from roughly 1,000 distinct speakers in in-the-wild monologue videos, with audio, text, and video modalities. We evaluate the six-class joint emotion classification task (happy, sad, angry, fear, disgust, surprise) using the official train, validation, and test splits, which provides a stringent test of generalization at scale. To prevent information leakage in every dataset, all prepro-cessing statistics (normalization means and variances, filter parameters) are estimated on the training fold only and applied unchanged to validation and test, and no subject, speaker, or session is shared across splits. No data augmentation is applied to the test sets. We implemented models in PyTorch 2.1 with mixed-precision training enabled for computational efficiency. Training utilized the AdamW optimizer [53] with decoupled weight decay. To support reproducibility, the manuscript specifies the complete preprocessing and alignment pipeline, the full physiological 1D-CNN architecture, the exact cross-validation protocols and seeds, the model-selection rule, and the per-method tuning budget. 

2) Modality-Specific Feature Extraction: For audio modality, we employ the Wav2Vec2-Large model [15] pretrained on 960 hours of LibriSpeech. We sample input waveforms at 16 kHz and extract the final layer hidden states (1024 dimensions), applying temporal mean pooling to obtain fixed-length repre-sentations. For text modality, IEMOCAP experiments use BERT-Base (uncased) [16] with 768-dimensional embeddings. For KEMDy20, we employ KoELECTRA-Base [52], which is pretrained on Korean corpora and better suited to this dataset. We tokenize text inputs with a maximum sequence length of 128 tokens and use the final [CLS] token embedding as the utterance-level representation. For physiological signals (EEG in DEAP; EDA/IBI/Temperature in KEMDy20), we adopt a unified 1D convolutional architecture. We normalize raw signals to zero mean and unit variance using statistics computed on the training fold. For EEG, we apply a bandpass filter (4-45 Hz) to isolate relevant frequency bands and segment signals into 1-second non-overlapping windows. For peripheral signals, we apply simple moving average smoothing with a 0.5-second window. The encoder is a 4-layer 1D-CNN with output channels [64, 128, 256, 512], kernel sizes [32, 16, 8, 4], stride 2 and “same” padding at every layer, each followed by batch normalization and a GELU activation. The resulting sequence of 512-dimensional vectors is passed through a single temporal self-attention block with 8 heads to capture long-range dependencies, after which global average pooling over time yields the final hphysio ∈ R512 representation. For CMU-MOSEI, audio and text use the same Wav2Vec2-Large and BERT encoders, and video uses the ViT [CLS] embedding, with utterance-level features obtained by mean pooling over the aligned frame sequence.

schedule reducing to 1 ×10 −6 over 50 epochs. For the annealing coefficient λt in the loss function (Eq. 16), we employ a linear warm-up from 0.01 to 1.0 over the first 10 epochs, following [17]. C. Comparison with Existing Methods 

We compare EMT-DS against recent multimodal fusion ap-proaches. The general multimodal baselines are: (1) TACFN [9], a transformer-based adaptive cross-modal fusion network; (2) CAG-MoE [13], a cross-attention gated mixture of experts that is the prior best method on KEMDy20; (3) ERTNet [58], an interpretable transformer for EEG emotion recognition; (4) DCT [55], a dual cross-modal transformer with multi-ple acoustic features; (5) Ensemble-DL [59], an ensemble deep learning approach for DEAP; and (6) the Multimodal Transformer (MulT) [25], a widely used directional cross-modal attention model. To compare directly against uncertainty-aware and evidential methods, we additionally include (7) trusted multi-view classification (TMC) [26], an evidential Dempster-Shafer fusion model that is the closest competitor to our setting, and (8) a variational Bayesian multimodal baseline (VMM-Dropout) that estimates predictive uncertainty through Monte Carlo dropout [46]. We additionally compare against four further methods: (9) MISA [60], which learns modality-invariant and modality-specific representations; (10) ConFEDE [61], which performs contrastive feature decom-position; (11) QMF [62], a quality-aware dynamic fusion method that weights modalities by estimated uncertainty; and (12) a 2026 method, the complementarity-supervised spectral-band routing network (Atsuko) [33]. QMF is evaluated on all datasets, MISA on the language-bearing ones, and ConFEDE and Atsuko on CMU-MOSEI for which they were designed; all are reimplemented under the same protocol. Temperature scaling, MC dropout, and deep ensembles are further analyzed as uncertainty baselines in Section IV-G. All baselines were reimplemented under a single unified protocol with identical feature extractors, data splits, and tuning budget; no numbers are quoted from the original papers, so the comparison is strictly controlled. All methods received an identical compute budget (Random Search, 50 trials per dataset), and results are reported as mean ± 95% confidence interval over five random seeds across the cross-validation folds. Table III presents the comparison. EMT-DS outperforms all baselines on every dataset, with the largest margins on datasets exhibiting high uncertainty and conflict, and consistently attains the lowest calibration error. On IEMOCAP, EMT-DS achieves 84.3% accuracy (UA) and 82.7% F1, surpassing the previous best method (DCT) by 2.6 points in accuracy while reducing ECE from 0.129 to 0.042. On DEAP, under the corrected leave-one-subject-out protocol, EMT-DS obtains 82.4% accuracy, a 4.3-point improvement over the strongest baseline (TMC, 78.1%); the subject-independent protocol yields lower absolute accuracy than the subject-dependent splits used in some prior work, but provides a leakage-free and more realistic estimate of generalization to unseen subjects. On the severely imbalanced KEMDy20, EMT-DS achieves 96.8% accuracy and 94.9% F1, outperforming CAG-MoE by 2.3 and 2.7 points respectively. Paired t-tests with Holm-Bonferroni correction confirm that the accuracy improvements of EMT-DS over the strongest baseline are statistically significant on all three datasets ( p < 0.01). Notably, the evidential baselines TMC and VMM-Dropout substantially close the calibration gap to EMT-DS (for example ECE of 0.082 and 0.068 on IEMOCAP) yet remain less accurate, indicating that the differentiable Dempster-Shafer fusion contributes beyond evidential encoding alone. The added baselines do not change the ranking: MISA reaches competitive accuracy but is poorly calibrated, while QMF, being uncertainty-aware, is better calibrated but less accurate than EMT-DS, which remains best on every dataset and metric.

K. Robustness to Modality Corruption 

To verify Lemma III.1 (Noise Immunity), we evaluate model performance under severe sensor corruption. We inject additive Gaussian noise N (0, σ 2) into the audio representations of the IEMOCAP test set, with σ ranging from 0.0 to 1.0. Figure 5 presents the results. Conventional Attention Fusion degrades rapidly (80.3% → 45.3%) because softmax normal-ization forces the model to attend to noisy features if they have large magnitudes. Early Fusion collapses even faster. In contrast, EMT-DS retains strong robustness, maintaining 71.2% accuracy even at σ = 1.0. As the audio signal becomes uninformative, the evidential head predicts high uncertainty ( uaudio → 1), causing the D-S fusion rule to automatically downweight the corrupted modality and rely on the clean text modality. This confirms that evidential fusion acts as a functional safety mechanism, not just a regularizer. Furthermore, the gradient-norm analysis (Supplementary Material) empirically verifies Lemma III.2: as noise increases (σ → 1), the gradient norm ∥∇θ LKL ∥ of EMT-DS collapses to zero, preventing the model from updating weights based on corrupted data. In contrast, the softmax baseline maintains high gradient variance, leading to noise overfitting. Additionally, we track gradient norms during training over 50 epochs to validate the Lipschitz bound from Theorem III.1. We observe that despite the theoretical worst-case bound of L ≈ 4 × 10 8,empirical gradient norms remain bounded below 10 3 throughout training, confirming that the softplus activation and KL regu-larization keep evidence magnitudes moderate ( ∥em∥1 ≈ 100 in practice). Consistent with this, the architectural evidence cap (Eq. 6, Emax = 1000) behaves exactly like the conflict clip (analyzed in the Supplementary Material): because the per-modality evidence norm never approaches Emax , the L1

projection is the identity on every observed sample, leaving accuracy and ECE unchanged while guaranteeing the finite Lipschitz constant of Theorem III.1. The cap is therefore a guarantee by construction rather than a tuned hyperparameter. Gradient clipping at threshold 1.0 is applied as an additional safety mechanism but is rarely triggered (less than 0.5% of batches).

M. Conflict-Triggered Selective Review 

We evaluate the Dempster-Shafer conflict ctotal as a signal for selective review, where the goal is to flag likely-misclassified samples for human inspection. This is a selective-prediction (error-triage) procedure rather than a full active-learning loop: it involves no iterative querying, relabeling, or retraining. To avoid optimistic bias, all operating points and risk-coverage curves are fit on a held-out calibration split and evaluated on a disjoint test split. Figure 7 plots the percentage of total errors detected against the fraction of data reviewed (sorted by decreasing conflict). Because the conflict loss (Eq. 17) trains the conflict to correlate with errors, part of the error-detection ability could in principle be an artifact of that objective. We therefore separate the intrinsic signal from the trained one. Table IX compares review strategies. The intrinsic Dempster-Shafer conflict of the model trained without the conflict loss, which depends only on belief-mass disagreement (Eq. 10) and never sees an error label, already detects 71.2% of errors within a 20% review budget, well above predictive entropy (58.3%) and random review (20.1%). Adding the conflict loss raises detection to 80.2% and lowers the area under the risk-coverage curve to 0.071. A directly supervised error-prediction (abstention) head, trained on the misclassification indicator, is marginally better at pure error detection (80.9%) but has worse risk-coverage (0.074), adds parameters, and yields no interpretable quantity beyond an error score. Conflict matches it with no added parameters, retains a fixed probabilistic meaning, and simultaneously governs the fusion. To test whether conflict reflects genuine semantic disagree-ment rather than only error, we annotated a random subset of 300 IEMOCAP test utterances for cross-modal incongruence on a three-point scale, independently of the model outputs. The Dempster-Shafer conflict correlates with these human incongruence labels at Spearman ρ = 0.57 ( p < 0.001), whereas the learned error head aligns with them only weakly ( ρ = 0.34). Conflict therefore tracks true cross-modal disagreement, not merely the error indicator it is partly trained on, and provides an interpretable basis for human-in-the-loop review. O. Computational Complexity Analysis 1) Theoretical Complexity: We analyze the computational complexity of EMT-DS and compare it with baseline methods. Let M denote the number of modalities, K the number of classes, N the sequence length, and d the embedding dimension. The forward pass complexity consists of three components. Each transformer encoder has complexity O(N2d + Nd 2) for self-attention and feed-forward layers, yielding O(M(N2d +

Nd 2)) for M modalities. The evidential head requires linear pro-jection to evidence space with complexity O(MdK ). Pairwise Dempster-Shafer fusion requires computing conflict and fused beliefs, with complexity O(MK 2) for conflict computation and O(MK ) for belief updates, totaling O(MK 2). The total complexity is O(M(N2d + Nd 2) + MdK + MK 2). For typical values ( N = 128, d = 768, K = 7, M = 4), the dominant term is O(MN 2d) from transformer encoders, making EMT-DS asymptotically equivalent to standard multimodal transformers while adding negligible overhead for D-S fusion. Compared to attention-based fusion which requires O(M2d2)

for cross-modal attention, our D-S fusion ( O(MK 2)) is more efficient since K ≪ d in typical scenarios.

EMT-DS improves over existing methods across all four benchmarks. On IEMOCAP the gains hold under session-independent cross-validation despite speaker-induced distri-bution shift, and the confusion matrix (Figure 2) shows balanced per-class performance (diagonals above 0.83). On DEAP the leakage-free subject-independent numbers are lower than the inflated subject-dependent results common in the literature but reflect genuine generalization to unseen par-ticipants. The advantage stems from Dempster-Shafer fusion explicitly modeling conflict: it attains the lowest conflict (0.126) among fusion strategies, whereas attention-based fusion shows 87% higher conflict (0.235), suggesting that learned attention weights may amplify rather than resolve disagreement. On the imbalanced KEMDy20, belief mass is allocated adaptively (text dominating for Neutral, audio for Angry), and the incremental and controlled ablations (Table V) confirm each component’s contribution. The model is robust across wide ranges of the KL anneal-ing coefficient, conflict weight, and learning rate (accuracy variance below 2.5%), and under zero-shot cross-dataset transfer accuracy degrades while conflict rises in step, so the model flags its own ignorance and acts as a domain-shift detector rather than failing silently (Supplementary Material). A distinctive advantage is interpretability: Subjective Logic opinions decompose predictions into belief masses, uncertainty, and base rates, unlike the scalar weights of attention, and the negative correlation between conflict and accuracy (Pearson 

r = −0.68) makes conflict a reliable signal for flagging samples for human review. The strengthened baseline set clarifies where the improve-ment originates. The strong softmax methods (MISA, Con-FEDE, and the 2026 spectral-band routing network) are competitive on accuracy but poorly calibrated, whereas the uncertainty-aware QMF calibrates well yet trails on accuracy; EMT-DS is the only method that lands on the better side of both axes at once. This separation is diagnostic. QMF reweights each modality by a scalar quality estimate but never represents cross-modal conflict, so it cannot express, or act on, the case in which two confident modalities disagree. EMT-DS instead carries a belief-mass opinion per modality together with a conflict coefficient of fixed probabilistic meaning, which simultaneously tightens calibration and yields a deployable abstention signal. The comparison with the softmax-then-Dempster-Shafer variant (Section IV-J2) and with recent post-hoc evidential wrappers reinforces the point: applying the combination rule to frozen outputs recovers only part of the benefit, while learning the fusion end to end, with gradients flowing through the conflict term and a supervised conflict loss, is what closes the accuracy-calibration gap. Because inference remains a single forward pass with a closed-form fusion step, these gains arrive without the sampling or ensembling cost of the Bayesian baselines, IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON AFFECTIVE COMPUTING 17 

which matters for the clinical and edge deployments that motivate trustworthy emotion recognition. A limitation is that Theorem III.2 assumes conditional independence between the modalities. The residual dependence, captured by the correlation ρ between modality calibration errors, is class-dependent (highest for the high-arousal Angry class, ρ = 0.28, and lowest for Neutral and Sad, ρ = 0.08 and 0 .11) but modest on average ( |ρ| = 0.16 ± 0.09), and the measured fused ECE stays low for every class, so the near-independence approximation is adequate in practice. Modeling this residual dependence explicitly, for instance through copula-based couplings of the modality posteriors, is left to future work. VI. C ONCLUSION 

This paper presented EMT-DS, which advances multimodal emotion recognition through formal uncertainty quantification and interpretable fusion. Grounding predictions in Subjective Logic and combining modalities via Dempster-Shafer theory yields explicit epistemic uncertainty and cross-modal conflict, an interpretable belief-mass decomposition, and efficient closed-form fusion. Across four benchmarks EMT-DS improves accuracy over the previous best methods, by 2.6, 4.3, and 2.3 points on IEMOCAP, DEAP, and KEMDy20 respectively, and by 2.4 points over the strongest evidential baseline on CMU-MOSEI, while reducing calibration error and adding only modest overhead (11% training time and 29% lower memory than attention-based fusion). The conflict term flags high-conflict samples for human review, which is essential for safety-critical deployment. A. Comparison with Uncertainty Quantification Methods 

To position EMT-DS against established uncertainty quan-tification techniques, Table I compares it on IEMOCAP against a softmax point estimate, post-hoc temperature scaling [1], Monte Carlo dropout [2] with 30 stochastic forward passes, a deep ensemble of five independently trained models, and an evidential model without Dempster-Shafer fusion (evidential heads with concatenation). All methods share the same frozen encoders. Deep ensembles attain competitive calibration and negative log-likelihood, but require five separate training and inference cycles, and MC dropout incurs a thirtyfold inference overhead. EMT-DS attains the lowest Expected Calibration Error (0.042) and the best risk-coverage area (AURC 0.071) at a single forward pass, while also being the most accurate. This shows that the quality of the uncertainty arises from the evidential and conflict-aware formulation rather than from sampling or ensembling cost.

C. Conflict-Loss and Numerical Sensitivity 

We provide explicit sweeps that justify the conflict-loss design choices. Table III sweeps the conflict threshold τ from 0.3 to 0.7. Accuracy and the selective-review error-detection rate both peak at τ = 0 .5, the midpoint of the conflict range, and performance is stable in a broad neighborhood. Table IV sweeps the conflict weight λc from 0 to 0.3; the chosen value of 0.10 lies in a stable region, and setting λc = 0 (no conflict loss) reduces accuracy to 82.8% and worsens calibration. Table V varies the numerical clipping constant used in the normalization factor. Accuracy and ECE are invariant from 0.99 to 0.9999, and the clip activates on at most 0.02% of fusion operations, confirming that it is a float-precision guard rather than a tuned hyperparameter. IV. C. End-to-End Cost Profiling 

Table VII reports the full end-to-end cost on IEMOCAP, including total wall-clock training time, the number of epochs to convergence under our early-stopping rule, per-sample inference latency, peak VRAM, and parameter count. Because the pretrained encoders are frozen, their features are extracted once and cached, so the per-sample feature-extraction cost during training is amortized to a negligible value. EMT-DS converges in 26 epochs and 1.4 hours, close to the concate-nation baseline (1.2 hours) and more than three times faster than CAG-MoE (4.5 hours), while its 8.7 GB peak memory remains suitable for commodity and edge GPUs.
