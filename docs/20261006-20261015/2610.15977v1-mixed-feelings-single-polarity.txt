Abstract 

Emotion datasets are routinely mapped onto positive and negative valence, by assigning each emotion category one polarity. We test where this works and where it fails, using SemEval-2018 Affect in Tweets (English, Ara-bic, Spanish), Affective Text aligned with EmoBank, raw GoEmotions annotations, nat-uralistic hashtag corpora, and a controlled an-notation study. Fixed projection works well when a text carries labels of one valence. It fails on a subset that can be identified from the labels alone: tweets whose emotion labels point in opposite directions. These are 9.0% of polar SemEval test tweets but cause 54.8% of the polarity errors of a learned projection, are more than twice as likely to receive a neutral-or-mixed gold label, and are not rescued by modeling the full label set. Annotators produce such configurations routinely: 16.3% of multil-abel GoEmotions judgments combine emotions from the dataset’s own positive and negative parents. In a controlled study holding word and reader constant, context changes the coarse valence judgment in 56.8% of cases, against 19.9% for controls. We recommend project-ing label configurations rather than labels, ab-staining or keeping a mixed class where labels conflict, and reporting coverage alongside ac-curacy. 

1 Introduction 

Suppose an emotion classifier assigns a tweet both 

joy and sadness , and a downstream system needs a single polarity. A category-to-valence lookup gives opposite signs to the two labels, leaving the tweet-level decision unresolved. In GoEmotions (Dem-szky et al., 2020), 16.28% of multilabel emotional judgments combine categories from the dataset’s positive and negative parents. SemEval-2018 Af-fect in Tweets (Mohammad et al., 2018) provides independently annotated text-level valence with which to assess what those labels predict. Fixed mappings can provide useful category-level priors without determining the polarity of every text. Mappings of this kind are widespread. Emo-tion datasets use small basic-emotion sets (Ekman, 1992; Plutchik, 1980), larger multilabel inventories such as GoEmotions (Demszky et al., 2020), or continuous dimensions such as valence, arousal, and dominance (VAD) (Russell, 1980; Buechel and Hahn, 2017). Combining or comparing them requires a mapping between schemes, often onto coarse valence. Corpora are harmonized through such mappings (Bostan and Klinger, 2018; Ko-ufakou et al., 2024; El-Haj and Takanami, 2023), and inventories are coarsened through them (Gong et al., 2024). The relevant claim is that an emotion category can be assigned a fixed valence across the items to which it is applied. This assumption is often implemented during preprocessing with-out being separately validated against item-level valence judgments. When it fails, distinct labels merge, predictions that were wrong become correct after projection, and information about intensity, context, and multilabel composition disappears. A classifier that predicts anger for a text annotated 

disgust , for instance, is wrong at the fine-grained level but correct once both are scored as negative. Let x denote the text unit being annotated (e.g., a word, sentence, or tweet), and let E(x) = e be an emotion label assigned to it by a human or model. Study 1 examines the information emotion la-bels provide about valence, how that information changes with co-occurring labels, and how the pat-terns extend to naturalistic emotion signals. We analyze SemEval-2018 (Mohammad et al., 2018), Affective Text headlines (Strapparava and Mihal-cea, 2007) aligned with EmoBank (Buechel and Hahn, 2017), raw GoEmotions annotations (Dem-szky et al., 2020), and two naturalistic hashtag cor-pora (Mohammad and Kiritchenko, 2015; Vishnub-hotla and Mohammad, 2022). A fixed lexical pro-jection assumes that an affective prior associated with a word or sense remains informative when that item occurs in context. Study 2 tests this assump-tion by comparing affective judgments of words in isolation with judgments of the same words in sen-tence contexts. Together they address three ques-tions: 

RQ1: How well does an assigned emotion label predict the valence of the same annotated text unit, and is this relation stable across lan-guages, datasets, and annotation designs? 

RQ2: How does text-level valence vary with co-occurring emotion labels, where does label-only polarity projection fail, and how much information is retained under the GoEmotions sentiment hierarchy? 

RQ3: With lexical item and reader held constant, how much does context change coarse va-lence, dominant emotion, and position in af-fective space? Across studies, category prototypes are informa-tive but do not reliably determine text-level valence, while context alters judgments of the same word. 2 Related Work 

Categorical and dimensional representations cap-ture related but non-identical affective structure (Ekman, 1992; Plutchik, 1980; Russell, 1980), and each can be recovered from the other with high accuracy: Buechel and Hahn (2018) map between VAD and basic-emotion ratings with near-human reliability, including cross-lingually, and Park et al.

(2021) predict VAD scores from corpora annotated only with categories. Recovering one representa-tion from another on average does not mean that a single category fixes the valence of every text carrying it. Harmonization nonetheless makes this step routinely: El-Haj and Takanami (2023) av-erage the VAD vectors of a sentence’s emotions into one target and threshold predicted valence into three classes, and Gong et al. (2024) map fine-grained inventories down to sentiment classes. Re-views caution that unified data must be read in light of differing emotion definitions and annota-tion procedures (Koufakou and Nieves, 2025), and that lexicon-derived affect scores are easily over-interpreted (Mohammad, 2016). Psychology gives reasons to expect variation within categories. Positive and negative affect can co-occur in experience, as in happiness and sad-ness, fear and enjoyment, or disgust and amuse-ment (Larsen and McGraw, 2014). This has led to bivariate measures that rate positive and nega-tive affect separately instead of asking for one net valence (Kreibig and Gross, 2017). If people can feel both at once, a coarse representation of a text may need to keep both as well. Emotion categories are connected by continuous gradients rather than separated by clean boundaries (Cowen and Keltner, 2017), appraisal approaches derive emotion from context-sensitive evaluations of events (Hofmann et al., 2020; Troiano et al., 2023), and distributed la-bel representations capture graded relations among categories (Wang and Zong, 2021). Lexical re-sources show the same: the NRC Emotion Lexicon pairs Plutchik categories with sentiment (Moham-mad and Turney, 2010, 2013), yet many entries de-pend on word sense and context (Zad et al., 2021), and classifiers degrade sharply once explicit lexical cues are removed (Sosea and Caragea, 2025). Annotation practice points the same way. Alm et al. (2005) split surprise into positive and neg-ative variants, multilabel resources such as XED let emotions co-occur within a text (Öhman et al., 2020), and category overlap is a recurring annota-tion problem (Öhman, 2020). GoEmotions makes the mapping explicit: its 27 categories come with a sentiment hierarchy based on Cowen and Kelt-ner (2017), and collapsing to sentiment or Ekman groupings improves classification (Demszky et al., 2020). That work does not claim that every judg-ment falls under a single parent, so we treat the hierarchy as a strong example of a useful coarse representation and ask how closely raw annotations 2respect it. Concurrent work asks whether same-named emotion labels are operationally equivalent across datasets (Öhman and Koufakou, 2026); we instead ask whether an emotion label determines the va-lence of the text it annotates. 

3 Study 1: Corpus evidence 

Study 1 evaluates emotion-to-valence inference us-ing independently annotated valence, co-occurring emotion labels, within-annotator judgments, and naturalistic hashtag signals. Bootstrap, null-model, and screening details are given in Appendix A. 

3.1 How informative are emotion labels about text valence? 

As part of SemEval-2018 Task 1, Mohammad et al. (2018) obtained separate annotations for cat-egorical emotion and continuous valence. The emotion-classification dataset (E-c) uses eleven la-bels (anger, anticipation, disgust, fear, joy, love, op-timism, pessimism, sadness, surprise, trust), while valence regression (V-reg) uses Best–Worst Scal-ing (Kiritchenko and Mohammad, 2017) to produce continuous scores on a 0–1 scale. Neither anno-tation is derived from the other. Matching items present in both datasets by identifier yields 6,563 tweets (2,355 English, 1,794 Arabic, 2,414 Span-ish), retaining the original train/dev/test splits. 3.2 What happens when emotion labels co-occur? Co-occurrence (SemEval-2018). For each or-dered emotion pair (e, c ), we compute 

∆Ve,c = V (e, c ) − V (e, ¬c),

the difference between the mean valence of tweets carrying both e and c and tweets carrying e with-out c. Thus ∆V > 0 means that tweets carrying 

e have higher valence when c is also present, and 

∆V < 0 means lower valence. Of the 110 possible ordered pairs, 68 had at least five e + c tweets in each language in the training data; 62 had the same sign of ∆V in English, Arabic, and Spanish. We tested these directional effects on held-out data: 46 retained the same sign in all three languages, and 45 had pooled 95% intervals excluding zero (Ap-pendix Tables 9–11). For example, anger tweets have mean valence .202 higher when joy is also annotated, whereas joy tweets have mean valence 

.199 lower when anger is also annotated. These are tweet-level associations and do not identify which span or emotion contributes the valence difference. 

What polarity projection loses. We evaluate label-only polarity projection on 2,076 held-out Se-mEval tweets with at least one emotion label, using official V-oc polarity as the target. A conventional lookup assigning nine emotions fixed signs and leaving anticipation and surprise unsigned covers 92.1% of tweets, with 20.0% errors among predic-tions across the three gold classes. Restricting eval-uation to gold-positive and gold-negative tweets, its error rate falls to 3.5% at 94.3% coverage. Fixed projection is therefore highly effective when the target has a clear polarity. Errors are concentrated in particular label con-figurations. Tweets containing both convention-ally positive- and negative-associated labels con-stitute 11.8% of the labeled test set, but 38.2% have neutral-or-mixed V-oc labels, compared with 16.4% of other tweets. A model averaging label-specific polarity priors makes errors on 33.6% of these opposed-label tweets with polar gold labels, against 2.7% of other polar tweets; among tweets with polar gold labels, opposed configurations con-stitute 9.0% of cases but account for 54.8% of the model’s wrong-polarity predictions. Estimat-ing priors for exact label sets gives nearly identi-cal performance. These results identify where the tested label-only projections are unreliable, includ-ing cases whose gold polarity is unambiguously positive or negative (Table 3).

Since the hierarchy is the dataset’s own, this is a test of GoEmotions against its own annotators.

3.3 Do the patterns extend to naturalistic emotion signals? 

Self-labeled hashtags give an independent natural-istic signal that no annotator assigned. We use the Tweet Emotion Corpus (TEC; Mohammad and Kir-itchenko, 2015), which contains 21,051 tweets col-lected with emotion-word hashtags. After remov-ing hashtags, URLs, and mentions, lexical valence separates joy from the pooled negative categories only moderately ( D = .333 ). As a second check, we analyze TUSC social-media posts (Vishnub-hotla and Mohammad, 2022) as distributed through ABCDE (Wahle et al., 2026). The strict set yields 332 analyzable posts (97 joy, 235 pooled negative). Residual lexical valence distinguishes joy from the pooled negative categories with AUC = .716 [95% CI .654 , . 776 ], D = .433 [.308 , . 552 ]. Among the 231 strict-set posts containing at least one extreme lexical cue ( |V | ≥ .70 ), 26.8% contain extreme cues exclusively opposite to the hashtag-derived valence. Full preprocessing details, per-emotion comparisons, and robustness checks are reported in Appendix D.

Across corpora, valence diagnosticity varies markedly by category, and text-level valence changes with co-occurring labels. The projection evaluation shows that conventional mappings per-form well on clearly polar texts, but errors concen-trate in opposed-label configurations. These con-figurations account for 11.8% of labeled SemEval test tweets, yet 54.8% of polar prediction errors un-der learned binary averaging. They are also more than twice as likely to receive neutral-or-mixed V-oc labels. GoEmotions supplies complementary evidence at the within-annotator level: 16.28% of multilabel emotional judgments combine positive-and negative-parent emotions. The hashtag corpora show only moderate lexical separation between joy and negative emotion signals. Study 2 shows the same thing one level down. The corpora show that a single text can carry op-posed emotions; Study 2 shows that a single word can carry opposed readings until context selects one, and that the selection goes both ways. With word and reader fixed, context switched coarse judgments at nearly three times the control rate, and did so by resolving mixed readings rather than by intensifying them. A collapse can be right on average and still misrepresent a large share of indi-vidual instances. Categorical-to-dimensional mappings are useful and often accurate on average (Buechel and Hahn, 2018; Park et al., 2021). However, averaging emo-tion vectors into one sentence-level VAD target, as in El-Haj and Takanami (2023), can obscure co-occurring opposites: joy and sadness may yield a neutral-looking average even when both are an-notated. Projection is a modeling choice whose information loss depends on the combination of labels. 

Implications for practice. Four consequences follow for emotion-label harmonization and eval-uation. (i) Use category-specific evidence. Ta-ble 6 groups categories by observed valence di-agnosticity; these are empirical priors rather than guarantees of item-level polarity. (ii) Account for label combinations. Opposed-label tweets are dis-proportionately difficult for the tested projection methods, even when their gold polarity is positive or negative. 6 Conclusion 

Emotion categories provide informative valence priors, but their reliability as text-level polarity pre-dictors depends on the categories and their com-binations. Conventional projection performs well on texts with clearly positive or negative gold va-lence, while opposed-label configurations account for a disproportionate share of prediction errors and frequently receive neutral-or-mixed valence labels. GoEmotions shows that individual annotators also select emotions across sentiment parents, and the controlled annotation study demonstrates that con-text can substantially change the affective interpre-tation of the same lexical item without increasing its intensity. Emotion-to-valence mappings should therefore be evaluated at the level of label config-urations, with explicit reporting of coverage, un-certainty, and the information lost through coarse projection. 

Limitations 

Study 1 is observational. In SemEval-2018, co-occurrence effects are properties of the annotated tweet: they may reflect distinct clauses, events, or spans and do not show that an emotion it-self changes valence when it co-occurs with an-other. Three languages do not establish broad cross-linguistic generality, and the Affective Text– EmoBank, GoEmotions, and hashtag analyses are English-only. 8The projection evaluation starts from gold emo-tion labels, so it gives an upper bound for label-based projection: a pipeline using predicted la-bels inherits these errors in addition to its own. The baselines are simple label-only methods and do not establish the best performance achievable from emotion labels; models that also see the text may resolve some opposed configurations. The V-oc classes are derived from continuous V-reg scores rather than elicited independently, and class 0 merges neutral and mixed valence, so we cannot separate texts that annotators found mixed from texts they found neutral. Opposed E-c labels ag-gregate judgments across annotators and need not mean that any single annotator selected both. The GoEmotions analysis audits a published hi-erarchy rather than comparing labels with an in-dependently elicited valence layer, and positive– negative co-selection may partly reflect dis-course composition that punctuation-based sen-tence counts only approximate. Raw judgments are dependent within items and raters, which we address with comment- and rater-cluster bootstraps; consensus-filtered rates are item-level and not di-rectly comparable with rater-level rates. Hashtag labels are self-assigned and noisy, the strict TUSC set is small and dominated by sadness, and lexical valence is a coarse proxy for text valence. Study 2 used a convenience sample of univer-sity students in a course setting, and completion varied: eight main-arm participants completed all 150 trials. We keep incomplete participants only where a valid within-participant pair exists and report participant-equal estimates alongside pair-weighted ones. The isolated target always came first; repeated targets were separated by at least eight trials and trial position showed no association with displacement, but order and prior exposure cannot be ruled out. The 50 targets were chosen to diagnose context sensitivity, not to represent En-glish vocabulary, and the mechanism groups are un-even (two register targets, four controls). Euclidean measures treat the wheel as a metric space, which we take as a property of the instrument rather than a claim about affective coordinates. Annotators did not rank their points, so the strongest association is derived from radial intensity rather than elicited directly. 

Ethical Considerations 

Study 2 participants were university students in a course taught by one of the authors. Before taking part, students were informed that the annotation activity formed part of an active research project and that their annotations could be used as data in a research publication. Research use was voluntary: students could opt out without consequence for their grade by completing an alternative task. The annotation platform collected no names, student numbers, or demographic information linked to the annotations; responses were associated only with non-identifying participant codes used to construct within-participant pairs. The task involved judgments of individual En-glish words and short invented sentences and con-tained no personal or sensitive questions, decep-tion, audio or video recording, or physical or psy-chological intervention. Diagnosticity and prototypes (SemEval-2018). 

Because D folds AUC around the chance reference of .5, its bootstrap interval is descriptive rather than a test of D = 0 . We use 95% percentile intervals from 5,000 item-level bootstrap resamples within each language; cross-language summaries average the three language-specific estimates within each replicate. Continuous prototypes are training-set mean V-reg scores. Polarity is evaluated separately using official V-oc classes, with 0 retained as neu-tral or mixed. The single-label continuous-valence projection test ( R2) compares each score with one prototype rather than an aggregate of several; the test subsets contain 70 English, 125 Arabic, and 247 Spanish single-label tweets. 11 Polarity projection (SemEval-2018). We join V-oc to E-c and V-reg by tweet identifier, language, and original split. Of 2,128 test tweets, 52 have no E-c emotion label, leaving 2,076 for evaluation. Conventional lookup assigns positive polarity to joy, love, optimism, and trust, negative polarity to anger, disgust, fear, sadness, and pessimism, and leaves anticipation and surprise unsigned; ties pro-duce abstention. Learned baselines estimate V-oc class distributions from training data, either averag-ing per-label distributions or smoothing exact-label-set distributions toward those averages. Binary pre-dictions select a polarity, whereas three-class pre-dictions can also select neutral-or-mixed. Opposed configurations contain at least one conventionally positive and one conventionally negative label. We report coverage and error conditional on prediction, and repeat evaluation on the 1,681 test tweets with polar gold labels. Predictions of class 0 are not treated as abstentions. These comparisons evalu-ate the specified label-only baselines, not an upper bound on achievable performance. For each ordered pair (e, c ) and language, we compute ∆Ve,c = V (e, c ) − V (e, ¬c). Training pairs require at least five e + c tweets per language and the same sign of ∆V in English, Arabic, and Spanish; held-out dev+test data assess whether that sign replicates. This is a directional discovery– replication design rather than a multiple-testing procedure; held-out intervals use 5,000 bootstrap resamples. 

Projection implementation settings. The label-based projection methods do not require neural-network training. Per-emotion and exact-label-set valence distributions are estimated from the Se-mEval training partitions. Exact-label-set estimates use ten pseudocounts with the averaged per-label distribution as the smoothing prior. This was a fixed reference setting, not the result of hyperpa-rameter optimization. Direction-stability estimates use 5,000 bootstrap resamples with random seed 20261011. Performance is evaluated on the orig-inal held-out test partitions without fitting to test labels. Error-rate intervals use the Wilson method. No hyperparameter search was performed for these projection baselines. 

Affective Text × EmoBank (Study 1B). We use the released combined EmoBank valence score as primary and repeat the analysis for reader- and writer-perspective valence. Because the original test partition is small for several emotions, the all-item analysis is primary. At ≥ 75 several categories become too sparse, so we do not use that threshold. 

GoEmotions (Study 1C). The ambiguous par-ent was left unpolarized by design, so the primary test is positive–negative co-selection; broader any-parent crossings are secondary. For rates over all emotional judgments, a cardinality-preserving per-mutation null (10,000 permutations) reshuffles la-bels while preserving judgment cardinality and approximately preserving label marginals. For multilabel rates, a matched null preserves the ob-served cardinality distribution and samples unique labels by their empirical frequencies; we generate 10,000 complete null datasets and compute one-sided Monte Carlo p-values (headline comparison 

p = .00010 ). Category-level purity uses a category-conditioned version of the same null. Depen-dence is handled with cluster bootstraps over com-ments and, separately, over raters (all-judgment rate: comment-cluster CI [3.74, 3.95], rater-cluster CI [3.23, 4.45]), and by removing the 1, 3, 5, and 10 most prolific raters. 16 Annotation instructions 

In this study, you will annotate the emotional associations of words and expressions. 

For each target word, you will first see the word by itself. Later, you will see the same word used in short sentences. When you see a sentence, use the sentence to understand the meaning and use of the highlighted word. However, focus your annotation on the highlighted word, rather than on the overall mood or emotion of the sentence. 

When considering the emotional associations of a word, think about both: 

● Emotions directly expressed or described by the word. For example, anger is directly associated with furious .

● Emotions that the word evokes, suggests, or is commonly associated with , even when the word does not directly describe an emotion. For example, home may evoke warmth, comfort, or happiness, while murder may evoke fear, sadness, or disgust. 

Thus, a word does not need to name or describe an emotion for you to associate it with an emotion. 

Emotion annotation 

Use the emotion wheel to show which emotion or emotions you associate with the highlighted word in that particular use .

● Place a point in the area of the wheel that best matches the emotion. 

● Distance from the centre shows intensity: 

closer to the centre means weaker ;

farther from the centre means stronger .

● You may place more than one point if the word is associated with more than one emotion. 

● Consider both: the emotions directly expressed by the word and emotions that the word evokes or suggests. 

● If several emotions come to mind, you may indicate all of them rather than choosing only one. 

For example, home might be associated with warmth, comfort, and happiness, even though it does not explicitly name an emotion. 

Sentiment Annotation 

Next, decide whether the highlighted word has positive or negative emotional associations in that particular use. 17 Choose one: 

● Positive: the word has positive emotional associations. 

● Negative: the word has negative emotional associations. 

● Both: the word has both positive and negative emotional associations. 

● Neither: the word has no clear positive or negative emotional associations. 

Choose Both only when both positive and negative associations are genuinely present. Do not choose Both simply because you are uncertain whether the word is positive or negative. 

Remember that you are rating the highlighted word , not the overall emotional tone of the sentence. A word may have positive emotional associations even when it appears in a negative sentence, or negative emotional associations when it appears in a positive sentence. 

Important 

Do not over think your responses. Let your instincts as a speaker of English guide you. 

Consider the emotions that the word itself brings to mind, including emotions it may evoke, suggest, or connote , not only emotions that it explicitly describes.
