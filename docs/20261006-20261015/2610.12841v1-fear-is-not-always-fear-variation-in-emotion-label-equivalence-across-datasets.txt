# Fear Is Not Always Fear: Variation in Emotion Label Equivalence Across Datasets

Abstract 

Emotion classifiers are routinely applied be-yond their training data, on the assumption that same-named labels such as fear or sur-prise mean the same thing across resources. We test this assumption for five labels shared by GoEmotions, XED, and BRIGHTER, com-paring models on identical inputs and under cross-corpus transfer. On the same texts, one 

fear classifier flags 71% as fearful and another 8%, yet the two largely agree on how the texts rank. Across 30 resource–label comparisons, training-label prevalence explains about half of this kind of scale mismatch, and a fitted intercept removes most of it. What recalibra-tion cannot repair is larger: transferred models lose 55% of native discrimination in aggregate, agreement in ranking between classifiers is only weakly related to that loss, and for surprise the resources disagree about which texts express the emotion. The choice of training resource can therefore change conclusions: on two exter-nal tweet collections, BRIGHTER- and XED-trained classifiers give opposite answers about which shows more surprise , even after rank normalization, with intervals excluding zero in both directions. A shared emotion label name does not establish an interchangeable measure-ment target. 

1 Introduction 

Emotion classifiers are increasingly used as mea-surement instruments: to track fear during a pan-demic, compare joy across communities, or detect sadness in clinical text (see e.g. Kleinberg et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Chu et al., 2025). A model fine-tuned on GoEmotions (Dem-szky et al., 2020) or XED (Öhman et al., 2020) is applied to new data, and its fear scores are read as measurements of fear. This practice rests on an assumption that is rarely tested: that a label such as 

fear refers to approximately the same target across datasets. This assumption does not always hold, and the ways in which it fails have different implications for calibration fixes. We test it by training one model with a separate output head for each resource and comparing the heads on the same held-out texts. On the same 2,765 BRIGHTER test texts, the BRIGHTER fear head classifies 70.8% as fearful, averaged over three seeds, while the GoEmotions 

fear head classifies only 7.6%, a more than nine-fold difference, using development-selected thresh-olds. Our prevalence analysis attributes much of this scale mismatch to base-rate differences, and the two heads largely agree on the rank ordering 

of the texts by predicted fear (ρ = .897 ): a study’s absolute estimate of fear can change dramatically with the training resource even when the models preserve nearly the same ranking of texts. For 

surprise , by contrast, the XED and BRIGHTER heads disagree substantially about the ranking itself. The same headline observation, “cross-resource dis-agreement”, therefore hides qualitatively different problems that require different remedies. Emotion resources differ in theoretical ground-ing (fine-grained taxonomies versus Plutchik-style basic emotions; Plutchik 1980), inventory size, an-notation guidelines, aggregation rules, and text do-main: a label called fear in a 28-category scheme competes with nervousness , while in an eight-category scheme it does not. Such differences could move the category boundary, change how confidently a model predicts the label, or alter which texts are judged most representative of the emotion. Existing work establishes that cross-corpus emo-tion classification is difficult (Bostan and Klinger, 2018) and that merging corpora into a unified in-ventory helps for some datasets but not others (Ko-ufakou et al., 2024). What it does not establish is what form these cross-resource differences take. “Poor transfer” can arise from several distinct mis-matches with very different practical consequences. 1arXiv:2610.12841v1 [cs.CL] 9 Oct 2026 A difference consistent with prior shift may in prin-ciple be corrected without target labels; a differ-ence in probability scale can be corrected with a small number of them; a disagreement about which texts express the emotion cannot be fixed by any order-preserving recalibration. This paper asks which of these mismatches ac-tually occur between same-named emotion labels, focusing on anger , fear , joy , sadness , and surprise .We make three contributions: 1. A framework for label equivalence compris-ing four progressively weaker relationships: intercept, positive affine, and nonlinear mono-tonic equivalence, and ordering divergence. 2. A controlled same-input comparison. Ashared encoder with dataset-specific heads scores identical texts, yielding a 30-unit empir-ical map of cross-resource compatibility (five shared labels × six directed resource pairs). 3. An empirical characterization of portabil-ity and repairability using independent cross-corpus transfer, progressive recalibration, a decomposition of probabilistic transfer error into miscalibration and discrimination loss, and corpus-level comparisons testing whether resource choice changes measurement conclu-sions. Same-named heads usually preserve substantial rank agreement while differing strongly in proba-bility scale, and training-label prevalence explains much of that scale mismatch; transferred models nevertheless lose 55.3% of native discrimination in aggregate, and surprise shows ordering disagree-ment that no monotonic correction removes. These differences can change substantive conclusions: the direction of corpus-level comparisons depends on which resource the classifier was trained on, in-cluding for surprise between two tweet collections external to all three training resources. We there-fore argue that shared label names should be treated as hypotheses about equivalence, and that the rele-vant question is not whether two labels match but what form of equivalence they exhibit. 1

2 Related Work 

Emotion labels are shaped by the annotation scheme used to elicit them as well as by the un-

derlying affective phenomenon. Annotations dif-fer with the perspective annotators adopt (Buechel and Hahn, 2017), are entangled with intensity and annotator confidence (Troiano et al., 2021), and depend on which aspect of an event is represented (Oberländer et al., 2020); corpora also differ in domain, scheme, and label inventory, and models trained on one do not transfer cleanly to another (Bostan and Klinger, 2018). More broadly, anno-tation design is increasingly treated as part of the task definition rather than a neutral mechanism for recovering a pre-existing label: prescriptive and descriptive paradigms legitimately yield different label distributions (Röttger et al., 2022), aggrega-tion can obscure systematic variation in interpreta-tion (Prabhakaran et al., 2021; Mostafazadeh Da-vani et al., 2022), and divergent emotion judgments are often legitimate rather than noise (Uma et al., 2021; Plank, 2022; Ohman and Koufakou, 2026). A dataset label is thus an operationalization of a con-struct, not evidence that the construct is measured consistently across resources (Bean et al., 2026). We make this distinction empirically testable for emotion labels by asking which forms of apparent equivalence (prevalence, probability scale, order-ing) survive a change of annotation resource. Koufakou et al. (2024) map heterogeneous emo-tion corpora into a common inventory and show that unified training has uneven effects across datasets. We take the complementary route: rather than collapsing resource differences, we retain each resource’s own label function and ask which as-pects of a nominally shared label are equivalent, and whether the differences are large enough to change corpus-level comparisons. Relative judgments are more reliable than abso-lute rating scales in affect annotation (Kiritchenko and Mohammad, 2017; Mohammad and Bravo-Marquez, 2017; Bagdon et al., 2024), so agreement in relative ordering need not imply agreement on an absolute scale; we extend this distinction from annotators to resource-level model behavior. Psy-chometrics draws a related distinction, under the heading of measurement invariance, between in-struments that rank respondents alike and those that also share thresholds (Van de Schoot et al., 2012); our analysis is operational, and we neither fit latent-variable models nor make formal invari-ance claims. Platt scaling (Platt, 1999) and isotonic regres-sion (Zadrozny and Elkan, 2002) are standard post-hoc calibrators. Miscalibration under distribution 2shift is well documented (Guo et al., 2017; Ovadia et al., 2019). EM-based prior adjustment (Saerens et al., 2002) corrects prevalence changes under the assumption that class-conditional distributions re-main unchanged.We use these methods to study 

repairability , i.e., how much transfer error progres-sively more flexible target-side correction removes, and the CORP score decomposition (Dimitriadis et al., 2021) to separate Brier-score differences into miscalibration and discrimination components. 

3 Levels of Label Equivalence 

Let pA(y | x) and pB (y | x) be the probabilities that two resources’ models assign to a same-named binary label y for text x, and let ℓ(·) denote the logit. We distinguish four operational relationships between two such prediction functions. For the first three, the hierarchy is nested when the affine slope is positive and the nonlinear map is monotone increasing: 

L1: Intercept equivalence. ℓ(pB ) = ℓ(pA) + b.The two prediction functions differ only by a constant shift in log-odds. This relationship is consistent with a pure prior shift under the usual label-shift assumptions, but an intercept relationship alone does not establish that those assumptions hold. 

L2: Positive affine equivalence. ℓ(pB ) =

a ℓ (pA) + b with a > 0. The resources preserve text ordering but differ in probability scale or boundary sharpness. 

L3: Nonlinear monotonic equivalence. pB =

g(pA) for monotone-increasing g (equiva-lently on logits). Ordering is preserved, but the probability scales are related nonlinearly. 

L4: Ordering divergence. No monotone-increasing g adequately relates the two prediction functions: the resources disagree about which texts receive relatively higher scores for the emotion. These relationships represent progressively weaker forms of equivalence; we use them as refer-ence relationships rather than assigning each empir-ical comparison to a discrete level. L1–L3 preserve the ordering of texts and differ only in how the two prediction scales are related, so rank agreement provides a complementary diagnostic; L4 permits the ordering itself to differ. Because an intercept relationship may arise from label-prevalence dif-ferences, we also compare the fitted L1 mapping with the log-odds shift implied by training-label prevalences. Target-side recalibration against gold labels (Section 4.4) addresses a different question, repairability, and does not by itself assign a transfer unit to a level: residual error after isotonic recali-bration is interpreted jointly with rank agreement, since residual error alone may also contain estima-tion noise. 

4 Experimental Setup 

4.1 Data 

We use the three English resources in Table 1. The main analyses are restricted to the five la-bels that all three share ( anger , fear , joy , sad-ness , surprise ). Multi-label items are retained as multi-label: each shared emotion is modeled as an independent binary target, so an item may be positive for more than one shared label. Only un-ambiguous aliases (e.g. happiness →joy ) are nor-malized. Labels that a resource does not annotate are masked rather than treated as negatives. We preserve the released train/development/test parti-tions for GoEmotions and BRIGHTER. Because the English XED data did not provide equivalent partitions, we create a deterministic 80/10/10 split by SHA-256 hash of normalized text, ensuring that duplicate normalized texts remain in the same partition. The training/development/test partitions contain 43,410/5,426/5,427 examples for GoEmo-tions, 21,761/2,810/2,631 for English XED, and 2,763/115/2,765 for English BRIGHTER. 

External collections. For the external measure-ment comparison, we use the English emotion-classification (E-c) data from SemEval-2018 Task 1 (Mohammad et al., 2018). The 2016 collection is the official training partition; the 2017 collection pools the official development and gold-labeled test partitions. Neither is used to fit or calibrate our models. After NFKC normalization, case fold-ing, and whitespace normalization, we remove one text overlapping our models’ training data and one within-collection duplicate, leaving 6,837 and 4,144 texts; no normalized texts are shared across the two collections. 4.2 Two model sources 

A standard transfer experiment confounds input domain, representation, prevalence, boundary, and semantics. We therefore use two complementary setups built from different models. 

Compatibility (same-input comparison). A sin-gle encoder is trained jointly with one output head per resource, and every text is scored by all three heads, so the evaluation input and encoder repre-sentation are held fixed when same-named heads are compared. Remaining differences reflect heads learned under different supervision and training distributions; they should not be interpreted as iso-lating annotation semantics alone. We report Spear-man correlation (ordering agreement) and mean absolute probability difference (MAE; scale agree-ment). 

Portability (independent transfer). Separately, we train one native specialist per resource and apply each to the other two test sets, giving six directed transfers × five labels = 30 transfer units. We re-port AUROC, average precision (AP; several target labels are sparse), F1, Brier score, and log loss. Ex-cess error is measured relative to the native target specialist evaluated under the same procedure. Because compatibility and portability come from different models, agreement between them is not an artifact of scoring a single model twice. 

Implementation. All models use 

FacebookAI/roberta-base (Liu et al., 2019) as the pretrained encoder. Training uses AdamW with a learning rate of 2 × 10 −5, weight decay 0.01 , a linear learning-rate schedule with a warmup ratio of 0.06 , maximum sequence length 192, training batch size 16, and evaluation batch size 32. Models are trained for at most three epochs with early stopping (patience 1) and gradient clipping at a maximum norm of 1.0. Multi-label predictions use sigmoid outputs and binary cross-entropy over labels observed for the corresponding resource; labels not annotated by a resource are masked from the loss. The shared-encoder model uses natural sampling, with each training item seen once per epoch. All reported model results are averaged over three random seeds (13, 42, and 87). 

Compute and software. The RoBERTa-base models contain approximately 125 million param-eters each; the shared-encoder model with three classification heads contains approximately 126 million. Experiments used NVIDIA GH200 GPUs with 120 GB of GPU memory. We estimate approx-imately 10 GPU-hours across training, exploratory runs, and evaluation, based on allocation records. 

4.3 Direct same-input mapping 

For each of the 30 directed resource–label com-parisons, we fit L1–L3 mappings between same-named heads on identical target-domain develop-ment texts and evaluate them on held-out target-domain test texts, without using gold emotion la-bels. L1 fits an intercept-only logit mapping, L2 a positive affine logit mapping, and L3 a mono-tone isotonic mapping; all minimize squared error in probability space. The source-to-target training-prevalence log-odds shift serves as a parameter-free L1 baseline. We report held-out MAE and Spear-man’s ρ. To distinguish systematic disagreement from training variation, residual MAE is normal-ized by the larger of the source- and target-head same-head cross-seed MAEs, a conservative seed-stability reference. 

4.4 Recalibration ladder 

For each transfer unit, we fit target-side correc-tions on target development data and evaluate them on target test data: (i) intercept-only logistic re-calibration; (ii) label-free prior-shift EM (Saerens et al., 2002), applied both to raw outputs and to outputs first calibrated on source development data; (iii) Platt scaling; and (iv) isotonic regression. 5 Same Labels, Different Operational Behavior 

Same-named heads (heads for the same-named la-bel across resources) mostly agree on ordering and disagree on scale. Figure 1a shows rank agree-ment for each directed resource–label compari-son; across these 30 comparisons, mean agree-ment is ρ = .756 (median .805 ), and only three fall below .50 . Table 2 summarizes probability-space mapping errors. A shift computed only from training-label prevalences cuts mean held-out MAE from .193 to .096 and improves 26 of 30 units; a development-fitted intercept reduces it further to 

.076 , improving on the prevalence shift in 20 of 30. Base rates therefore account for much, but not all, of the scale relationship. Affine and monotonic mappings add little on average. Figure 1b shows the residual after monotonic mapping relative to the conservative seed-stability reference; these ratios average 1.21 (median 1.13). 

Fear illustrates a predominantly scale-based mis-match. On BRIGHTER texts, the BRIGHTER and GoEmotions heads preserve high rank agree-ment ( ρ = .897 ) despite a large probability-scale difference. The converse comparison on GoE-motions texts has more moderate rank agreement (ρ = .609 ), but its raw MAE of .377 falls to .012 af-ter monotonic mapping, within conservative same-head seed variation. Combining GoEmotions’ ner-vousness category with fear does not explain the mismatch with BRIGHTER: MAE stays at .517 under max pooling ( .517 ) and reduces it only to 

.507 under noisy-OR, while slightly lowering rank agreement. For surprise , ordering itself diverges more strongly (Figure 1). The weakest comparison is XED →BRIGHTER, with ρ = .329 ; its mono-tonic residual remains 2.97 times the conservative same-head seed-stability reference. The reverse BRIGHTER →XED comparison also has weak rank agreement ( ρ = .368 ), although its post-monotonic residual falls within same-head seed variation. This ordering divergence is consistent with L4-type incompatibility: an increasing mono-tonic mapping cannot reconcile different rankings. 

Joy and anger in XED →BRIGHTER likewise re-tain substantial residual disagreement after mono-tonic mapping (2.41 and 2.03 times the conserva-tive reference, respectively). Same-named labels therefore do not share a single form of equivalence: probability-scale mismatch is widespread, while a smaller set of comparisons also exhibits substan-tial ordering divergence. 6.2 Probabilistic transfer error 

Probability-scale disagreement between compati-bility heads closely tracks the excess Brier error of independently trained transferred models across the 30 units (Spearman ρ = .973 ). This correspon-dence is partly structural (Appendix C), so we treat it as convergent evidence that the scale disagree-ment seen in the same-input comparison reappears as probabilistic transfer error, rather than as an in-dependent predictive result. The CORP decomposition shows that most ex-cess probabilistic error is associated with miscali-bration, but discrimination loss is also substantial. Averaged across the 30 transfer units, excess Brier score is .112 : .088 is excess miscalibration and 

.024 is loss of discrimination. Relative to the native target model’s discrimination, transferred models lose 55.3% in aggregate (median unit: 63.0%); 22 of 30 transfers lose at least half. The loss is espe-cially pronounced for surprise , for which 81.7% of native discrimination is lost across transfers. The native reference models are also not ob-viously limited by fixable training-label errors: LLM-assisted revision of flagged training labels yielded no consistent native improvement across three seeds (Appendix H).

8 Resource Choice Can Reverse Corpus Comparisons 

To test whether these differences matter for down-stream measurement, we compared mean emotion scores between each pair of held-out corpora under each of the three heads. The direction of the corpus comparison depends on the head in 8 of 15 corpus-pair–label comparisons. Five of these reversals disappear after converting each head’s scores to pooled percentile ranks, indicating that they arise from probability-scale differences; three remain (Appendix F). Because these corpora are also the domains from which the models were learned, this analysis shows that measurements depend on the training resource, not how often such reversals would occur on arbitrary external corpora. To examine whether reversals extend beyond the heads’ training domains, we compared the ex-ternal SemEval-2018 collections defined in Sec-tion 4.1. 9 Discussion and Conclusion 

Prevalence and calibration account for most of how same-named emotion labels differ in level, but not for how they differ in ranking, and ranking differ-ences are what survive the corrections a careful user would apply: they appear as lost discrimina-tion after transfer and as a reversed comparison af-ter rank normalization. A shared label name should therefore be treated as a hypothesis of operational equivalence, not evidence of it. 

Practical recommendations. (1) Check whether training-prevalence differences explain an apparent scale mismatch before attributing it to deeper con-struct differences. (2) When target labels are avail-able, begin with intercept-only recalibration; Platt scaling adds only modest average improvement, and required sample sizes are target-dependent, par-ticularly for sparse labels. (3) Check both rank agreement on shared inputs and discrimination against target annotations: low rank agreement warns of ordering differences that monotonic recal-ibration cannot repair, while high agreement alone does not establish target-label validity. (4) Test whether substantive conclusions are robust to the choice of training resource. 

Future work. A formal measurement-invariance analysis with latent-variable models is a natural ex-tension. BRIGHTER also annotates 28 languages under a single scheme, so the same-input design can test whether label equivalence holds across lan-guages when guidelines and label inventory are held fixed. 8Limitations 

We study three English resources, five labels, and one encoder family (RoBERTa-base); the pattern of relationships may differ for other languages, resources, or architectures. Our unit of analysis is small (30 directed label-transfer units drawn from three undirected dataset pairs), so correla-tions over these units are descriptive, and our ro-bustness evidence rests mainly on per-seed replica-tion and leave-one-out analyses. The same-input comparison holds evaluation texts and the shared encoder fixed, but the resource-specific heads are learned from different supervision and training dis-tributions; it therefore shows that the heads dif-fer, not whether the cause is annotation guidelines, label semantics, or domain. The L1–L4 frame-work characterizes relationships between predic-tion functions, while target-side calibration probes repairability and does not itself assign a transfer unit to a level. We analyze the five shared labels in-dependently rather than modeling their multilabel structure jointly. The BRIGHTER compression analysis (see Appendix E) is observational; its ini-tial association with transfer error disappears after matching on the released five-label configuration. 

Ethical Considerations 

This study is a secondary analysis of existing text annotations; we recruited no participants and col-lected no new personal data. We did not conduct an additional systematic audit for personal iden-tifiers or offensive content, and do not claim that the source texts are anonymized or screened. Our planned release contains analysis code and aggre-gate results; source texts and item-level predictions are excluded, and users are directed to the original data providers. Emotion predictions describe model responses to text under a particular annotation scheme. They should not be interpreted as individual psycholog-ical assessments or used for consequential deci-sions about individuals without separate validation. Dataset and model licenses are documented in Ap-pendix B.

D Few-Shot Recalibration 

We repeat both intercept-only and Platt recalibra-tion using target-development samples of 25, 50, and 100 items, together with the full development set, capped at the available development-set size. Random samples containing only one class fall back to the uncalibrated model; we additionally use a label-stratified diagnostic condition that guar-antees both classes. Because sample requirements differ substantially by target dataset, results are reported by target rather than only as an aggregate. Few-shot requirements differ sharply by target (Table 8). For XED, about 50 random target an-notations approach full-development performance. Sparse GoEmotions labels require larger random samples because small samples often contain no positives. When both classes are guaranteed, how-ever, 25 GoEmotions examples already approach full-development performance, showing that class coverage drives much of the sample requirement. For BRIGHTER, residual excess error changes lit-tle as the calibration sample grows, indicating that its remaining transfer gap is not primarily a small-sample calibration problem. Intercept-only recali-bration follows the same qualitative pattern as Platt scaling. 

E BRIGHTER Annotation-Compression Robustness Analysis 

We examined whether information lost when indi-vidual BRIGHTER annotations are projected into the released label space could explain the compara-tively large transfer discrepancies into BRIGHTER. Individual annotations were recovered for 5,623 of 5,643 English items (mean 8.5 raters per item). Among the recovered items, 49.0% show at least one diametric affect pattern in the raw annotation space, compared with 38.2% in the released repre-sentation. Mean pairwise label-set Jaccard agree-ment increases from 0.572 to 0.594 after projection. In total, 608 items (10.8%) contain diametric struc-ture in the raw annotations that is absent from the released representation; we refer to these as raw-only-diametric items. 

Unadjusted association. On the BRIGHTER test set, 296 of 2,765 items are raw-only-diametric. Table 9 compares their item-level excess trans-fer error with the remaining test items. Before recalibration, raw-only items show greater ex-cess error for both GoEmotions →BRIGHTER and XED →BRIGHTER. The difference is no longer evident after Platt or isotonic calibration.
