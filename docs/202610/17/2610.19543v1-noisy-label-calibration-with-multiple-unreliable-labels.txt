Abstract 

In medical annotation and crowdsourcing, multiple annotators assign labels to each example, but these labels may be incorrect. Treating such noisy labels as ground truth can bias calibration evaluation. To address this problem, we propose methods for evaluating and recalibrating a fixed binary predictor under the underlying clean-label distribution, using only noisy annotations. First, given annotator-specific noise probabilities, we propose Noise Corrected ECE (NC-ECE), a binned estimator of expected calibration error (ECE) under class-conditional noise. We derive non-asymptotic bounds on the estimator’s bias. Then, we propose practical algorithms for estimating unknown noise probabilities under conditional independence and label-orientation assumptions. We also develop loss-based and histogram post-hoc recalibration with clean-risk and calibration guarantees, respectively. Experiments on semi-synthetic benchmarks show that the proposed ECE estimator closely tracks its clean-label oracle counterpart and that the recalibration methods substantially improve latent-label calibration, whereas uncorrected recalibration against noisy annotations can degrade it. 

# 1 Introduction 

Using probabilistic predictions for decisions requires more than accurate class predictions: the probabilities should also be calibrated. Calibration means that predicted probabilities agree with outcome frequencies among examples assigned those probabilities. For example, among examples assigned an eighty percent probability of a positive outcome, about eighty percent should be positive [Guo et al., 2017, Vaicenavicius et al., 2019]. Calibration evaluation and post-hoc recalibration usually treat held-out labels as ground truth [Guo et al., 2017, Fujisawa and Futami, 2025]. In medical annotation and crowdsourcing, however, multiple annotators label each example, and these labels may contain errors [Raykar et al., 2010, Tanno et al., 2019]. Medical experts may disagree in their annotations [Gulshan et al., 2016, Elmore et al., 2015], and majority voting does not necessarily eliminate annotation errors [Krause et al., 2018]. We study how to evaluate and improve a fixed binary predictor’s calibration under the underlying clean-label distribution when only noisy annotations are available. Noisy-label evaluation confounds predictor miscalibration with annotator response tenden-cies [Dawid and Skene, 1979, Penso et al., 2024]. For example, excess positive responses can reflect either underpredicted probabilities or an annotator’s tendency to report positives. With a fixed number of annotators, more evaluation examples generally leave this bias intact; averaging or majority vote need not remove it either. Recalibration toward annotation agreement can even worsen true-label calibration. Reliable uncertainty assessment is important in medical decision support, where expert judgments may disagree [Mimori et al., 2021]. Noisy evaluation labels complicate predictive reliability assessment. Loss correction for learning with noisy labels provides our starting point. Known noise probabilities can yield unbiased estimators of clean-label risk [Natarajan et al., 2013, Patrini et al., 2017, van Rooyen and Williamson, 2018]. We apply this principle to calibration evaluation 1arXiv:2610.19543v1 [stat.ML] 14 Oct 2026 in Section 3. Under annotator-specific class-conditional noise, inverting each known channel yields a corrected label whose conditional expectation given the prediction matches that of the true label. Subtracting the prediction gives a corrected residual with the desired conditional mean (Lemma 1). This gives unbiased estimates of clean-label bin residual moments, whose absolute values are summed to form a noise-corrected binned ECE estimator (Definition 4). The required noise probabilities, however, are usually unknown. Response frequencies mix annotator errors with true-class proportions and need not identify either [Hui and Walter, 1980]. Multiple annotations of the same example supply additional information [Dawid and Skene, 1979, Allman et al., 2009]. Under conditional independence and label-orientation assumptions, we use their joint distribution, supplemented where necessary by score-defined groups. Section 4 characterizes identification conditions and constructs channel estimators accordingly, using two annotators with score-defined groups (Theorem 2) or three or more informative annotators. We then account for their estimation errors in ECE evaluation rather than treating estimated noise probabilities as exact. Our analysis connects noise-probability estimation to calibration evaluation under the clean-label distribution, separating bin approximation, sampling variation, and channel-estimation errors (Theorem 1). The same corrected labels support post-hoc recalibration in Section 5: corrected-loss fitting provides a clean-risk guarantee, while histogram fitting provides a calibration guarantee (Proposition 2). Both evaluation and recalibration thus address the underlying clean-label distribution using noisy annotations alone. Our contributions are threefold. 1. Calibration evaluation. We propose a noise-corrected binned ECE estimator with separate binning, sampling, and channel-estimation error bounds (Theorem 1), including sample reuse with fixed bins (Proposition 1). We also optimize annotator weights to reduce residual variance (Lemma 2). 2. Recalibration. Corrected-loss and projected histogram fitting provide clean-risk and calibration guarantees, respectively (Section 5; Proposition 2). 3. Empirical validation. Controlled and semi-synthetic experiments assess estimation and recalibration (Section 6). Human annotations show how label choice can reverse recalibration assessments. 

Related work. Calibration evaluation and binning bias have been studied extensively [Vaice-navicius et al., 2019, Roelofs et al., 2022, Futami and Fujisawa, 2024]. Weighted test functions provide a common view of calibration criteria [Błasiok et al., 2023, Gopalan et al., 2022]. Un-biased class-conditional loss correction is also established [Natarajan et al., 2013]. Kiryo et al. [2026] estimate ECE from positive and unlabeled data, and Futami and Ishida [2026] develop decontamination-based multicalibration estimators and post-processing for weak supervision. We build on this correction principle. Our focus is the statistical link from unknown, annotator-specific rates in repeated annotations to ECE evaluation and recalibration, including finite-sample error propagation and sample reuse. For identification, our two-group construction instantiates the Hui–Walter setting of two imperfect tests in populations with different prevalences [Hui and Walter, 1980]. Here the populations are observable score groups. The identification structure is classical. Our analysis connects quantitative channel-estimation guarantees in this setting to latent ECE. With additional annotators, we use the classical Dawid–Skene product-mixture model [Dawid and Skene, 1979, Allman et al., 2009]. Joint noisy-label classifier training [Tanno et al., 2019] estimates a predictor together with the annotation model. Our analysis evaluates and recalibrates a fixed predictor. Disagreement-based approaches model the distribution of annotator judgments [Mimori et al., 2021, Riera-Marín et al., 2026]. Our target is calibration to one latent binary outcome. The 2distinction between correcting known channels and identifying unknown ones determines the assumptions and scope of our results.

# 7 Conclusion and Limitations 

Corrected annotations enable ECE evaluation and recalibration from noisy responses. Our bounds separate binning, sampling, and channel-estimation errors, including fixed-bin sample reuse. Corrected-loss and histogram fitting provide clean-risk and calibration guarantees, respectively. Our framework assumes score-invariant error rates within each true class. Identifying unknown channels additionally requires conditional independence of annotators given the true label and score, and label orientation. Even under these strong assumptions, small channel gaps can amplify correction variance, while weak separation between group class proportions can destabilize two-annotator estimation. Likelihood rates need not hold after finitely many EM steps. Addressing the limitations of these assumptions and extending the framework to multiclass labels remain directions for future work.

# D Channel Identification and Estimation 

Identification asks whether two parameter values can produce the same distribution of available data. Estimation asks how accurately the uniquely determined parameters can be recovered from a finite sample. We address these questions in that order. The channel-error bounds can then be inserted into the calibration bounds of Appendix C. Within each model, θ denotes a candidate parameter vector, θ0 its true value, and ̂ θ an estimate based on Dch . The coordinates of ̂ θ corresponding to aj , b j are the channel estimates ̂ aj ,̂ bj used in Section 3.

Remark D.1 (What EM establishes) . Exact E- and M-steps do not decrease the likelihood [Dempster et al., 1977, Wu, 1983]. Neither monotonicity nor a finite number of initializations guarantees the global objective condition or the consistent local solution required above. Aconverged admissible fit is a numerical output, not a proof of global optimality or of consistency. A numerical fit at a boundary can also fall outside the regular interior theory. 32 D.7 Model checks and extensions 

For two annotators and L > 2, 3 L moments versus L + 4 parameters give 2 L − 4 restrictions at regular points. A marginal model has 2 K − 1 observable probabilities and 2 K + 1 parameters; 

K > 3 permits additional model checks. Discrepancy tests need their own regularity and calibration, especially near boundaries or weak identification. Group-specific channels require informative overlap and a common orientation in every group; effective sizes are the group counts. Missing answers require a missingness model and identifiable overlap. No guarantee for arbitrary selective missingness, dependence, or instance-dependent channels is asserted.

G.4 CIFAR-10N stress test 

CIFAR-10N supplies 50 ,000 images and three response columns [Wei et al., 2022]. Labels are mapped to animal (bird, cat, deer, dog, frog, horse) versus vehicle (airplane, automobile, ship, truck). CLIP ViT-B/32 [Radford et al., 2021] predicts animal probability by summing the six animal probabilities after softmax over ten prompts a photo of a {class} , without fine-tuning. Original CIFAR-10 labels [Krizhevsky, 2009] provide the evaluation reference. Response columns are treated as slots because workers can vary across images or batches. Disjoint pilot, evaluation-channel, and test partitions contain 10 ,000, 20 ,000, and 20 ,000 images, retaining the original ten-image annotation batches. Five pilot splits provide channel-fitting, recalibration, and validation subsets. Marginal Dawid–Skene EM estimates channels from responses alone; Joint score-bin EM is a separate comparison. Corrected labels are aggregated using IV weights. Joint EM uses five equal-width channel groups, ten starts, at most 1000 iterations, tolerance 10 −8, and converged fits with positive gaps. Within each family, Naive and Corrected methods share fitting examples and use average responses and IV-weighted corrected labels, respectively. Temperature and Platt minimize logarithmic loss with 0 .05 ≤ T ≤ 20 or −10 ≤ u, v ≤ 10. Fixed-bin fitting uses ten equal-width original-score bins and projected mean targets; empty bins receive the projected mean of all fitting targets. The auxiliary Joint map uses the five fitted prevalences on the pilot channel sample. Validation NC-ECE compares Base and the six Naive/Corrected candidates. Table 3 shows the fixed-bin comparison, and Table 8 shows all candidates. The evaluation-channel sample supplies a common correction after fitting. For each map g, ten equal-width bins of g(Z) are used on the test sample; Oracle, Soft-vote, and NC-ECE share predictions and bins, changing only the label targets. The five pilot fits share evaluation-channel and test samples; reported sample SDs measure variation across pilot fits.

Class-conditional loss correction uses known transition probabilities to recover clean risk [Natarajan et al., 2013]. Calibration studies also address noisy-label training, noisy validation labels, and noisy predictions [Olmin and Lindsten, 2022, Bae et al., 2022, Penso et al., 2024]. Positive–unlabeled ECE estimation [Kiryo et al., 2026] and weakly supervised multicalibration [Futami and Ishida, 2026] use other supervision structures. Our repeated annotations support estimation of unknown annotator-specific channels and propagation of that estimation error to ECE and recalibration. The identification routes specialize the Hui–Walter and Dawid–Skene models [Hui and Walter, 1980, Dawid and Skene, 1979, Allman et al., 2009]; the GMM analysis quantifies their stability [Hansen, 1982]. Unlike joint predictor-and-annotator training [Tanno et al., 2019], the predictor here is fixed independently of channel estimation and recalibration. Disagreement-based methods can instead treat differing judgments as target uncertainty [Mimori et al., 2021, Riera-Marín et al., 2026]. Our model presupposes one meaningful binary outcome, so disagreement must be interpretable as imperfect measurement rather than equally valid alternative labels. 

H.2 Scope 

Known marginal-channel correction permits annotation dependence; identification requires the assumptions in Section 4. Adding annotators does not establish conditional independence. ECE-estimation accuracy, small recalibrated ECE, and classification accuracy are distinct. Corrected-loss guarantees concern clean risk, histogram guarantees concern calibration, and IV optimality concerns known-channel variance under its stated conditions. Human-label results stress-test the method without verifying its noise assumptions or clinical validity. Benchmark labels serve as evaluation references; shared evaluation samples limit interpretation of pilot-fit SDs. 43
